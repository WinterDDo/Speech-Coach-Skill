# What the masters share

Read this when diagnosing a draft, when justifying a structural decision, or when the
user asks *why* a rule exists.

Two thousand years separate Aristotle from Nancy Duarte. A Baptist preacher in Memphis
and a Cupertino product launch have nothing culturally in common. Yet when you strip
the surface, the same small set of laws keeps reappearing — arrived at independently,
by people who never read each other. That convergence is the evidence. What follows is
organized by law, with who found it and what breaks when it's violated.

---

## The stance: design backwards from the audience's change of state

Before any law, a posture. Almost every weak speech is written from *"what do I want to
say?"* Every strong one is written from *"what is different about these people when I sit
down?"*

Aristotle organized rhetoric around what the audience must **decide** — that's why he
split it into deliberative (future action), forensic (past fact), and epideictic
(present values). The genre is defined by the listener's job, not the speaker's topic.
Duarte's inversion is the modern version: the **audience is the hero**, the speaker is
the mentor who hands them the torch. Not the reverse.

Practically: write the change first, in this form —

> Before: they believe/feel/do ____ . After: they believe/feel/do ____ .

Anything in the draft that doesn't move that delta is cut, no matter how good it is.
Especially if it's good — the best irrelevant material does the most damage, because it
gets remembered instead of the point.

## Law 1 — One idea, or none

**Who found it.** Aristotle: every speech advances a single proposition. Cicero's
*inventio* starts by fixing the *status*, the one point genuinely at issue. Barbara Minto:
a single governing thought sits at the apex, everything below supports it. Chris Anderson
at TED: the **throughline** — if you can't state it in about fifteen words, you don't
have a talk. Carmine Gallo's analysis of Steve Jobs: every launch had a single
headline short enough to be a news chyron ("Today, Apple reinvents the phone"). The
Heath brothers' "Simple": find the core, then be brutal about excluding everything else.

**Why it works.** A listener cannot re-read. Working memory holds one organizing
structure, and everything either hangs from it or falls out. Ten points delivered means
zero points retained — not because the audience is inattentive, but because nothing was
privileged, so nothing was encoded.

**Failure mode.** The "everything I know about X" talk. It feels generous. It is
actually a refusal to do the hardest part of the work — deciding — and it transfers that
work to the audience, who won't do it.

**Test.** Someone hears this once. Tomorrow at dinner, they describe it in one sentence.
Write that sentence. If you can't, don't write the speech yet.

## Law 2 — No gap, no attention

This is the engine. Attention is not given; it is loaned against the promise of
resolution.

**Who found it.** Aristotle's *stasis* — locate the point of disagreement, or there's no
speech to give. Monroe's Motivated Sequence puts **Need** as step two, before any solution
is offered: you must make them feel the problem before they will accept your answer.
Duarte's "sparkline" describes great speeches as a relentless oscillation between *what
is* and *what could be* — the tension is not a phase, it recurs throughout. The Pixar
story spine turns on "…until one day." George Loewenstein's information-gap theory of
curiosity gives the cognitive account: curiosity is the felt deprivation of a piece of
information you've become aware you lack.

**Why it works.** Interest isn't a property of a topic. It's a property of an *unresolved
state* in the listener's head. Your first job is not to inform. It is to make them want
the information.

**Failure mode.** Opening with your conclusion, your agenda slide, or your credentials.
Nothing is at stake, so nothing is attended to, and everything after that is fighting
uphill. Also: the flat middle — a speech that opens a gap, closes it at minute three, and
then coasts for twelve.

**Test.** At any point in the beat sheet, ask "what does the audience currently want to
know that they don't yet?" If the answer is "nothing" for more than ~90 seconds, you've
lost the room there.

> Note the tension with Law 1 in business settings: Minto says answer first, executives
> hate suspense. Both are right for their contexts. See "Where they disagree" below.

## Law 3 — You must earn the right to speak

**Who found it.** Aristotle's *ethos*, which he ranked as possibly the most potent of the
three appeals. Dale Carnegie made it the foundation of his entire method: **earn the
right to talk about your subject** — speak only about what you have lived or genuinely
studied, because conviction can't be faked and everything else in his system depends on
it. Brené Brown's contribution is the modern inversion: admitted vulnerability builds
credibility faster than displayed competence.

**Why it works.** Audiences make a fast, mostly unconscious judgment about whether you
have standing, and they make it early. Once they've decided you don't, your evidence stops
being processed as evidence.

**In practice.** There are three sources of standing. Name yours in the first two minutes:

- **I lived it** — I was in the room, it happened to me, I paid for it.
- **I studied it** — I've spent years looking at this and here's what I found.
- **I'm accountable for it** — this is my decision and my name is on it.

Obama's 2004 convention keynote spends its opening minutes on his father herding goats in
Kenya and his grandfather's service — establishing standing before advancing a single
argument. Brown opens by calling herself a researcher who had a breakdown. Neither is
decoration; both are credentials in narrative form.

**Failure mode.** Borrowed authority — citing experts instead of showing your own contact
with the problem. Also its opposite: an unearned, over-long, self-admiring biography.
Standing is established by a *specific* fact, not a résumé.

**Caveat.** Vulnerability works when it's about a wound that has closed. An open wound
makes the audience responsible for the speaker, which reverses the relationship and is
uncomfortable for everyone.

## Law 4 — One named person beats a thousand statistics

**Who found it.** The Heath brothers' "Concrete." Paul Slovic's research on the
identifiable-victim effect found something worse than "statistics are weak": adding a
*second* child to an appeal reduced giving relative to one child, and adding statistical
context reduced it further. Aggregation actively suppresses the response. Reagan's
speechwriters put a single named hero in the gallery of the 1982 State of the Union — an
innovation every subsequent president has copied, because it worked. Hans Rosling built
a career on turning global development statistics into physical boxes on a stage.

**Why it works.** Human moral and attentional machinery is built for individuals and
scenes. Abstraction is a summary of feeling that has already happened; it can't cause the
feeling.

**In practice.** Obama's Philadelphia race speech makes its argument not through
sociology but through Ashley Baia, a young organizer whose mother got cancer, and an
older Black man in the room who explains he's there "because of Ashley." The entire
thesis lands in a fifteen-second scene between two named people.

**Rule.** Every abstraction owes the audience one instance. The abstraction is your
*conclusion*; the instance is the evidence the ear will actually accept. Deliver the
instance first, the abstraction second — not the other way around.

**Failure mode.** "Engagement improved 23% across segments." Nobody can picture it, so
nobody stores it.

## Law 5 — The ear needs scaffolding the eye does not

A reader can go back. A listener cannot. So a speech must carry its own structure out
loud, in a way that would look repetitive on the page and sounds like emphasis in a room.

**Who found it.** Churchill worked this out at twenty-two in an unpublished essay, *The
Scaffolding of Rhetoric* (1897), naming rhythm, accumulation of argument, analogy,
correctness of diction, and what he called wild extravagance — passion. He had already
concluded that spoken persuasion has *physical, acoustic* properties. Classical rhetoric
catalogued the same devices as *elocutio*. Every great speech in English uses them
heavily.

**The load-bearing four.**

- **Tricolon (rule of three).** "Of the people, by the people, for the people." Three is
  the smallest number that establishes a pattern, and a pattern is what the ear can hold.
- **Anaphora.** Repeating the opening words of successive clauses. King's "I have a
  dream" runs eight times; Churchill's "we shall fight" runs five in a single passage.
  Repetition is how a spoken idea gets underlined.
- **Antithesis.** "Not X, but Y." Meaning arrives faster through contrast than through
  definition.
- **The callback.** A phrase planted early and returned at the end. This is what makes an
  audience feel a speech *closed* rather than merely stopped. It is the highest
  return-on-effort device in the entire toolkit.

**Failure mode.** Prose written for the page and then read aloud. Nested subordinate
clauses, careful qualifications, no repetition — literate, and completely inaudible.

## Law 6 — Write for breath

**Who found it.** Churchill again: "blood, toil, tears and sweat" — four words, all
monosyllables, all Anglo-Saxon. Lincoln's Gettysburg Address is 272 words and about ten
sentences. The Anglo-Saxon-over-Latinate preference in English oratory is centuries old
and holds: *use* not *utilize*, *end* not *terminate*, *buy* not *procure*.

**Why it works.** The listener decodes in real time with no ability to pause. A sentence
that requires holding a clause in memory to find out where it lands costs the listener
the next sentence too. Long sentences don't just fail — they cascade.

**In practice.**

- One idea per sentence.
- Vary length deliberately. Long sentences carry; short ones land. Put the short one last.
- Never require the listener to hold an open clause across more than a few words.
- Read aloud, standing, at speaking volume. Anything you stumble on is broken — the
  stumble is data, not clumsiness.

## Law 7 — Land on an image or an ask, never a summary

**Who found it.** Monroe's fifth and final step is **Action** — a specific, concrete
thing to do. Duarte's arc ends at what she calls the "new bliss," a picture of the
transformed world, higher than where the talk began. King does not summarize his
grievances; he goes to a mountaintop and lets freedom ring from every hillside. Jobs
ended launches with "one more thing."

**Why it works.** Recency. The last thing they hear is the thing they carry out of the
room, and it is disproportionately what the speech becomes in memory. Spending it on a
table of contents is a waste of the single most valuable ten seconds you have.

**The two legitimate endings.**

1. **The picture** — the changed world, made specific and sensory.
2. **The ask** — one action, small enough to do this week, named precisely.

Pick one. Doing both usually dilutes both.

**Failure mode.** "So, in conclusion, we covered three things…" The energy dies audibly.
Second worst: trailing off into "…so, yeah, that's it. Questions?" Write the last sentence
first if you have to; never improvise it.

## Law 8 — Delivery is not decoration

**Who found it.** Cicero's five canons — *inventio, dispositio, elocutio, memoria,
pronuntiatio* — make delivery co-equal with invention. Demosthenes, asked for the three
most important things in oratory, is said to have answered "delivery, delivery, delivery."

**The three highest-leverage things.**

- **The pause.** The most underused tool in speaking. Silence after a sentence tells the
  audience *that one mattered* more reliably than any emphasis. Amateurs fear it because
  it feels ten times longer on stage than in the room.
- **Memorize the edges, improvise the middle.** First thirty seconds and last thirty
  seconds verbatim; everything between them by beat. This is what produces a talk that
  sounds *spoken* rather than recited, while protecting the two moments where failure is
  most expensive.
- **Rate.** Nerves accelerate everyone. The felt correct pace on stage is faster than the
  actual correct pace.

**On the popular delivery science — be careful.** Two claims circulate constantly in
presentation advice and both are shakier than their popularity suggests:

- The **"7-38-55 rule"** (that communication is 7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language) is
  a misreading of Albert Mehrabian's experiments, which tested how people judged *feelings
  from single spoken words* in conflicting-signal conditions. Mehrabian himself has said it
  doesn't generalize. Don't repeat it.
- **Power posing**'s hormonal effects have largely failed to replicate; a weaker
  self-reported "felt power" effect has more support. The practice is harmless — the
  confident claim about cortisol is not.

Delivery matters enormously. It just doesn't matter for the reasons those two claims say.

---

## Law 9 — Cut, then cut again

At Gettysburg, Edward Everett spoke for two hours as the main event. Lincoln followed
with 272 words. One of those speeches is memorized by schoolchildren.

Nearly every draft improves when 15–20% is removed. Cut whole beats before trimming
adjectives — a shorter version of a bad structure is still bad. And running over time is
not a scheduling error; it is an unauthorized claim on other people's time, and audiences
register it as exactly that.

## Where they disagree

Consensus isn't total, and pretending it is produces bad advice. The disagreements are
usually context collisions — both sides right for their own room.

**Slides.** Jobs used them constantly and beautifully. Bezos banned them at Amazon in
favor of six-page narrative memos read in silence. Many of the best TED talks use none.
*Resolution:* slides are for what the ear cannot carry — an image, the shape in data, a
demo, a face. They are not for text, and never for your notes.

**Scripting.** Obama delivered from teleprompter, verbatim, because a president's exact
words are the deliverable. TED coaches speakers to memorize whole talks. Carnegie was
adamantly against memorizing word-for-word, arguing it kills the sense of live thought.
*Resolution:* script verbatim when the words are the artifact (legal, political, heavily
quoted, translated). Work from a beat sheet when connection matters more than precision.
Never half-memorize — that's the worst of both, and it's visible.

**Emotion.** Aristotle warned against *pathos* untethered from *logos*. Modern talk
culture over-corrects into personal narrative as a genre requirement. *Resolution:*
emotion earns attention; evidence earns belief. You need both, in that order, and a talk
that is all feeling gets described afterward as "moving" by people who can't say what it
argued.

**Humor.** Widely recommended, not actually universal. Gettysburg has none. Churchill in
June 1940 has none. *Resolution:* humor is a tool for warmth and for lowering the
speaker's status to the audience's level. It is not a requirement, and a forced joke in
the first minute costs more than it earns.

## A cultural caveat

This canon is overwhelmingly Greco-Roman and Anglo-American. The laws about attention,
concreteness, and structure appear to travel well. The *conventions* do not:

- **Directness of the ask.** Explicitly naming what you want lands as clarity in the US
  and as pressure in much of East Asia, where the ask may be better implied or deferred.
- **Self-deprecation.** Reads as confident and likeable in British and American settings;
  can read as genuine incompetence in more hierarchical or credential-oriented rooms.
- **Conclusion-first.** Standard in American executive settings; in high-context cultures,
  arriving at the conclusion after establishing shared ground can be the respectful order.
- **Personal disclosure.** The TED-standard vulnerability opener is a strong culturally
  specific convention, not a universal one.

When the audience's culture differs from the speaker's, treat the laws as portable and the
conventions as things to check.
