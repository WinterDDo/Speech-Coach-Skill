# Mining the speaker for material

Read this at Stage 2, every time.

This is the stage that decides whether the output is a real speech or competent filler,
and it is the one stage that cannot be done without the user. A language model can supply
structure, rhythm, and craft. It cannot supply the afternoon in 2019 when the deal fell
apart, or the name of the technician who found the bug, or what the customer actually said
on the phone. Those are the only things in a speech that no one else could have given.

**The economics of this stage:** ten minutes of good questions here saves an hour of
revision later, because a draft built on real material needs polishing, and a draft built
on nothing needs replacing.

## The hard rule

**Never invent the speaker's experience.** Not a plausible-sounding anecdote, not a
representative customer, not a "for example, imagine you're a manager who…" dressed up as
something that happened. If a beat needs personal material that hasn't been supplied,
write the placeholder:

```
[YOUR STORY HERE — needs: the moment you realized the old process was failing.
 ~40 seconds. Include: where you were, who else was there, what was said.]
```

A speaker who discovers on stage that their own anecdote isn't true has been damaged by
this skill. Structural suggestions are yours to make. Their life is not.

Illustrative hypotheticals are allowed when *labeled as hypothetical in the speech itself*
("suppose a team of four ships weekly…"). That's a rhetorical device, not a fabricated
memory. The line is whether the audience will believe it happened.

## How to run this stage

Don't hand over a questionnaire. Ask three or four questions, listen to what has energy in
the answer, and follow that. The signal you're looking for: the user starts giving detail
you didn't ask for. That's the material. Everything they answer briefly and dutifully is
not.

Start here:

> "Before I write anything — tell me about a specific time this actually came up for you.
> Not the general pattern. One occasion, with a date and a room."

## The extraction prompts

Use as many as the conversation supports. Grouped by what they yield.

### Standing — why you (Law 3)

- What have you personally paid for this knowledge — in time, money, or reputation?
- What do you know about this that someone reading the same reports would not?
- When did you first realize this mattered? What were you doing?

### The scar — the highest-value material there is

- When did you get this badly wrong? What did it cost?
- What did you believe about this five years ago that you now think was naive?
- What's the mistake you watch other people make because you made it first?

> A speaker who opens with their own failure has bought credibility that no credential
> confers. It also disarms the audience's resistance, because you've already said the
> thing they were preparing to think.

### The turn — the moment of change

- Was there a specific moment you changed your mind about this? What caused it?
- What piece of evidence was it that finally moved you?

> The turn is the most reusable material in the entire toolkit, because the audience is
> currently where you *used to be*. Walking them through your own conversion is more
> persuasive than arguing from your current position — you're not asking them to lose an
> argument, you're showing them a path you already walked.

### The face — one named person (Law 4)

- Who is one specific person affected by this? What's their name and what do they do?
- What did they say, in their own words? Do you remember the phrasing?
- Who's the person who'd be most helped if this room does what you're asking?

### The object

- Is there a physical thing you could hold up? A printout, a prototype, a broken part, a
  letter, a photo?

> Physical objects on stage are radically underused. Rosling used cardboard boxes for
> global income distribution. An object collapses an abstraction into something everyone
> sees at once, and it gives the audience's eyes somewhere to go.

### The numbers

- What's the one number that surprised *you* — not the number that supports your case?
- What's the number this room already believes, that turns out to be wrong?
- Can you express your key number as a comparison? (Not "40,000" but "everyone in this
  building, twice over.")

### The resistance

- What's the objection you're most afraid of? Who in the room will make it?
- What's the strongest version of the argument against you?
- What would a smart, well-informed person who disagrees with you say?

> Steel-manning the opposition in your own speech is one of the fastest credibility moves
> available. An audience that hears you state their objection *better than they would*
> concludes you have thought about this properly, and lowers its guard. Concede the real
> point; the concession is what makes the rest believable.

### The private version

- If you had ten seconds alone in an elevator with the decision-maker, what would you say?
- What do you believe about this that most people in that room don't?
- What's the thing you'd say if there were no consequences for saying it?

> These questions surface the speech the person actually wants to give, which is usually
> better and always more alive than the one they think they're allowed to give. Some of it
> won't be sayable. Get it on the table anyway — the sayable version of a real conviction
> beats the fully-sayable version of nothing.

## The gate

Before Stage 3, you need **at least two concrete first-hand items** — a named person, a
dated scene, an object, a scar, or a number that surprised the speaker.

If the user genuinely can't supply them right now, say so directly rather than proceeding
quietly:

> "I can build the structure now, but two of these beats need something from your own
> experience that I can't invent. I'll mark them clearly and we can fill them in before
> you rehearse. The talk will be about 70% of what it could be until then."

Then proceed with marked placeholders. Don't stall the whole project on it — but don't
paper over it either, because a draft with invisible holes is worse than a draft with
visible ones.

## Sorting what you get

Not all material is equal. Sort it before architecting:

| Grade | What it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | First-hand, specific, surprising, emotionally live | Opening, or the turn at the center |
| **B** | First-hand but ordinary, or specific but second-hand | Supporting a build |
| **C** | General knowledge, statistics without a story, borrowed examples | Use sparingly; never in the opening |

A single A-grade item will carry an entire five-minute speech. Six C-grade items will not.
If everything you've collected is C-grade, go back and ask again — usually the A material
exists and the user didn't think it was relevant, because to them it's just their job.

## When the speaker isn't the source

Sometimes the speech is being written for someone else (a CEO, a client), or the topic is
genuinely impersonal. Then:

- Interview the actual speaker if at all possible, even briefly. Twenty minutes on the
  phone is worth more than any amount of research.
- If you can't, mine the *organization's* material: incident reports, customer support
  transcripts, the origin story, internal war stories, the first customer.
- Failing that, use publicly documented cases with attribution — and accept that the talk
  will be informative rather than moving. That's a legitimate outcome for some occasions.
  Say so, rather than faking warmth.
