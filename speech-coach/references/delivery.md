# Rehearsal and delivery

Read this at Stage 7, or whenever the user asks about nerves, timing, Q&A, or virtual
presenting.

Cicero counted delivery as one of the five canons, co-equal with inventing the argument.
Demosthenes, asked to name the three most important things in oratory, is said to have
answered "delivery, delivery, delivery." A well-built speech delivered badly loses to a
mediocre speech delivered well, and everyone who has sat in an audience knows this.

---

## What to memorize

**Verbatim: the first thirty seconds and the last thirty seconds. Nothing else.**

- The **opening** because nerves peak in the first half-minute and cognition is worst
  exactly when the stakes are highest. A memorized opening runs on autopilot and buys you
  the time to settle.
- The **closing** because it's the line you will be least able to improvise and most costly
  to fumble.
- **The middle by beat, not by word.** You should know the *order and purpose* of your
  beats cold, and choose the sentences live.

This is why half-memorization is the worst state: a speaker reciting a remembered text
looks like someone retrieving, not thinking, and the audience can see it. Carnegie was
emphatic about this — word-for-word memorization kills the sense of live thought.

**The exception.** Script verbatim when the exact words are the deliverable: legal or
regulatory statements, political speeches, anything that will be quoted or translated,
anything where a misspoken sentence is a real cost. Then rehearse enough that the script
stops sounding like reading.

---

## The rehearsal ladder

Five passes. Each has a different job. Doing the same pass five times is not rehearsal.

| Pass | Where | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Read** | Sitting, quietly | Find broken sentences. Rewrite as you go |
| **2. Stand** | Standing, out loud, full volume | Find breath problems and dead beats. Time it |
| **3. Beats** | Standing, notes only — no script | Prove you know the structure. Where you get lost is a structural flaw, not a memory flaw |
| **4. Full** | Standing, with slides/clicker, timed | The real run. Note where you drift over time |
| **5. Hostile** | With one person, who interrupts | Q&A, and recovering your place after interruption |

**Pass 3 is the one people skip and the one that matters most.** If you lose your way when
the script is gone, the problem is that the beats don't follow from each other — the
audience would have gotten lost at the same place. Fix the structure, don't memorize harder.

**Time every pass.** Runtime creeps up live, not down: nerves add pauses, the room adds
laughs and questions, the tech adds delays. If it fits exactly in rehearsal, it will not fit
on the day.

## The pause map

Mark pauses in the script explicitly. Speakers who don't plan them don't take them.

Pause **after**:
- Your thesis sentence.
- Any number you want remembered.
- A rhetorical question — long enough for them to actually answer it internally.
- Your closing line. Especially there.

Pause **before**:
- The reveal.
- The ask.
- Any sentence where you're about to change register.

**Length.** Two to three seconds is right and will feel like eight on stage. This is
universal and reliable: the felt duration of silence while presenting is several times its
real duration. Trust the count, not the feeling.

Silence after a sentence is the strongest available signal that the sentence mattered. It
costs nothing and almost nobody uses it.

## Timing control

- Build to **85% of the limit**. The remaining 15% gets consumed by the room.
- Mark **`[CUT IF SHORT]`** on specific beats in the script, chosen in advance. Under time
  pressure you cannot make good cutting decisions live; make them now, in cold blood.
- Set **two checkpoints**: "by minute 4 I should be at the reveal," "by minute 12 I should
  be starting the close." A clock is far more useful than a nervous internal estimate.
- If you're running long, **cut a whole beat**. Do not speed up. Speeding up destroys every
  pause you planned and signals panic to the room.
- Going over time is not a scheduling error. It's an unauthorized claim on other people's
  time, and audiences register it as exactly that — often as the main thing they remember.

## Nerves

**What's true.** Stage anxiety is physiological arousal — adrenaline, raised heart rate,
narrowed attention. It is not a signal that anything is wrong, and it is not visible to the
audience at anything close to the intensity the speaker feels it. Speakers routinely
overestimate how much their nervousness shows.

**What helps, with reasonable support:**

- **Reappraisal over suppression.** Saying "I'm excited" rather than "calm down" performs
  better in experiments (Alison Wood Brooks, 2014) — arousal is easier to relabel than to
  eliminate, because the physiology of excitement and anxiety are nearly identical.
- **Longer exhale than inhale.** Four in, six out, for a minute. Vagal, reliable, and works
  while you're waiting to be introduced.
- **Arrive early and occupy the space.** Stand where you'll stand. Walk the room. Talk to
  three people who will be in the audience — you'll then be speaking to people you've met.
- **A memorized opening.** The strongest single intervention. The worst thirty seconds are
  survivable when you don't have to compose during them.
- **Rehearse standing, at full volume.** Rehearsing in your head trains nothing physical.

**What to be skeptical of.** "Power posing" produced widely-shared claims about hormonal
changes that have largely failed to replicate; the weaker "felt sense of power" effect has
better support. Do it if it helps you — just don't believe the cortisol story. And the
"7-38-55 rule" is a misapplication of Mehrabian's research on judging *feelings from single
words*; it does not mean words are 7% of communication.

**What doesn't help.** Alcohol. Picturing the audience naked. Trying to be someone else's
kind of speaker. Memorizing the whole script as insurance — it makes blanking more likely,
not less, because there's now one exact right word to lose.

## Voice and body, briefly

- **Volume:** aim at the back row. Nearly everyone under-projects, and under-projection
  reads as low confidence in the argument itself.
- **Pace:** nerves accelerate everyone. The pace that feels correct on stage is faster than
  the pace that is correct. Deliberately slow the first minute.
- **Eyes:** land on one person, finish a full thought, move. Sweeping the room continuously
  connects with nobody. Find three friendly faces spread across the room and rotate.
- **Hands:** at rest, down and open. Gesture when it's real; a gesture that illustrates
  something (size, direction, sequence) is worth ten that just move.
- **Feet:** plant them. Move with purpose between beats — walking to a new spot is a good
  physical signal for a section change — then stop. Constant drifting reads as anxiety.
- **The close:** stop moving entirely. Feet still, hands down, eyes up.

## Q&A

Q&A is where prepared speeches are most often undone, and it's the part almost nobody
rehearses.

**Prepare the five hardest questions.** Not the five most likely — the five you least want.
Write the answers out. The one you're dreading is the one that will be asked, and being
visibly ready for it is worth more than the answer itself.

**Answer in PREP:** Point, Reason, Example, Point. Thirty to sixty seconds. Long answers in
Q&A lose the room faster than anything in the talk proper.

**Buy thinking time honestly** by repeating or reframing the question. It confirms you heard
it, lets the room hear it, and gives you three seconds. It is not a stalling tactic; it's
standard practice.

**"I don't know" is a strong answer** when followed by a commitment: *"I don't know. I'll
find out and send it to you by Thursday."* Bluffing is detected far more often than
speakers believe, and it costs the credibility the whole talk just built.

**Bridge, don't dodge.** Answer the question that was asked, then connect it to your point.
Visibly pivoting away from a question is worse than a weak answer.

**Hostile questions.** Separate the challenge from the person. Agree with whatever part is
true — genuinely, first — then address the rest. *"You're right that we missed the last two
deadlines. Here's what's different about this one."* Never match hostility; the room is
watching your composure more closely than your content, and composure is the answer.

**The rambling non-question.** Let them finish, extract the one question inside it, answer
that: *"I think the question underneath that is whether we can afford it — let me take
that."*

**End Q&A on your own terms.** Take the last question, answer it, then re-deliver a
compressed version of your close. Never let the session end on someone else's tangent —
recency applies to the whole event, not just the speech.

## Room and tech checklist

Thirty minutes before:

- [ ] Walk the room. Stand at the back — can you read your own smallest slide?
- [ ] Test your clicker, with fresh batteries. Know where the "blank screen" key is (`B`).
- [ ] Test audio if you have any. Video with sound is the most common live failure.
- [ ] Confirm your aspect ratio on the actual projector — 16:9 into a 4:3 projector crops.
- [ ] Have the deck in two places: laptop and a USB stick or cloud link.
- [ ] Export a PDF as a fallback. Fonts and animations fail; PDFs don't.
- [ ] Know where you'll stand, and where the timer or clock is.
- [ ] Water, within reach, opened.
- [ ] Phone off — not silent, off.
- [ ] Know who introduces you and what they'll say.

## Virtual delivery

Different medium, different rules. Presenting to a screen the way you'd present to a room
is the most common virtual failure.

- **Camera at eye level.** A laptop on a desk shoots you from below, which reads badly.
  Raise it until the lens is level with your eyes.
- **Look at the lens on the lines that matter** — the thesis, the ask, the close. Looking at
  faces on screen means looking down and away to everyone watching.
- **Light from in front,** not behind. A window at your back makes a silhouette.
- **Shorter beats, more signposting.** You cannot read the room, so you have to be explicit
  about structure: "That's the first of three. Here's the second."
- **Say names.** "Priya, this next part is really for your team." Direct address is the only
  substitute for eye contact online, and it's a strong one.
- **Build in interaction every few minutes** — a poll, a chat prompt, an actual question.
  Attention decays much faster with no social pressure to stay present.
- **Stand if you can.** It changes your voice and your energy audibly, even seated-frame.
- **Assume someone's audio will break.** Have the deck ready to send, and don't lose your
  place when it happens.

## When things go wrong

- **You blank.** Stop. Look at your notes. The silence is much shorter than it feels. If
  you have no notes, say the last thing you're sure of out loud — "so, the second problem"
  — and the next beat usually returns. Audiences forgive a pause completely; they remember
  visible panic.
- **The tech dies.** Keep going without it. This is why you memorized the structure and not
  the slide order. A speaker who continues smoothly without slides gains credibility.
- **You're losing the room.** Change mode. Ask a question, move physically, drop to a
  story, or take a break. Do not just talk louder into it.
- **You've run long.** Skip to your pre-marked cuts and deliver the close in full. Never
  cut the close to save time — landing badly costs more than any beat you'd have kept.
- **Someone is openly hostile.** Stay level, agree with what's true, offer to continue after.
  The room is grading your composure, not the exchange.
