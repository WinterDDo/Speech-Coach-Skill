# Openings and closings

Read this when writing or repairing the first or last thirty seconds.

These two stretches are worth more than the rest combined. The opening decides whether the
audience listens to the middle. The closing decides what the middle *becomes* in memory.
They are also the two places where nerves are highest and improvisation is worst — so they
are the only parts that should be written word for word and memorized.

**The first job of an opening is not to introduce. It is to make them want the next
sentence.**

---

## Seven openings that work

### 1. The scene
Drop them into a specific moment. Time, place, sensory detail. No preamble.

> "It's 2:15 in the morning and the phone is ringing. It's the night manager at our
> Guangzhou plant, and he's telling me the line has been stopped for six hours."

*Use when:* you have A-grade first-hand material. This is the default choice — it satisfies
Law 2 (gap), Law 3 (standing), and Law 4 (concrete) in one move.
*Requires:* an actual event. Do not fabricate one.

### 2. The number that shouldn't be true
One surprising figure, alone, unexplained for a beat.

> "Ninety-four percent of the people who start our onboarding never finish it. Not most.
> Ninety-four."

*Use when:* the audience believes things are basically fine. The number's job is to break
that.
*Requires:* the number to genuinely surprise. A number that confirms what they think is a
weak opening.

### 3. The question with a wrong obvious answer
Ask something the room will answer confidently — and wrongly.

> "How many of you think our biggest competitor is [X]? … It isn't. It's the spreadsheet
> our customers already have open."

*Use when:* your talk reframes something. Pair with antithesis.
*Careful:* only ask for hands if you can survive an unexpected result, and only ask a
question whose answer you'll actually address. A rhetorical question you never resolve
reads as filler.

### 4. The confession
Open on your own failure.

> "For two years I gave this presentation with the wrong number on slide four. Nobody
> caught it. That's not the embarrassing part — the embarrassing part is why nobody caught
> it."

*Use when:* the audience is skeptical, senior, or has heard from you before. It buys
credibility (Law 3) and lowers your status to theirs, which makes the argument feel like a
conversation rather than a pitch.

### 5. The direct challenge to a shared belief
Name what everyone in the room believes, then say you think it's wrong.

> "Everyone in this company believes we lost the enterprise deal on price. I've read the
> transcripts. We didn't."

*Use when:* you have the evidence to back it and the standing to say it. High risk, high
return. Without evidence immediately following, this is just provocation.

### 6. The object
Hold something up. Say nothing for a moment.

> *[holds up a printed form, 14 pages]* "This is what we ask a new customer to fill in
> before they can spend a dollar with us."

*Use when:* you have a physical prop and a room that can see it. Massively underused, and
it gives every eye in the room a place to go during your most nervous ten seconds.

### 7. The stakes, stated flatly
No drama. Just the size of what's on the table.

> "We have about nine months of runway. What we decide in this room today determines
> whether there's a company in 2027."

*Use when:* the room is senior, time is short, and theatrics would read as manipulation.
Also the right opening for a crisis.

## Never open with

| Opening | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Hi, I'm ___ and today I'm going to talk about…" | Spends your most valuable seconds on information that's already in the program |
| An agenda slide | Tells them the shape of the box before they care what's in it. Nothing is at stake |
| Your credentials, listed | Standing is earned by a specific fact, not a résumé (Law 3) |
| An apology, or a technical difficulty | Draws attention to a problem the audience had not noticed |
| A joke unrelated to the talk | If it lands you've spent goodwill on nothing; if it dies you start from behind |
| "Can everybody hear me?" | Handle this before you begin. Your first sentence should be your first sentence |
| A dictionary definition | Universally recognized as a sign that the writer had nothing |
| Thanking a long list of people | Do it briefly *after* the opening lands, if at all |

**On the housekeeping problem.** If you genuinely must cover logistics — fire exits,
recording, timing — do it *after* your opening move, not before. Hook first, admin second.
The order costs nothing and changes everything.

## Six closings that work

### 1. The image
Paint the changed world. Sensory, specific, present tense.

> "A year from now, a technician in Monterrey opens this app, sees one number, and knows
> exactly what to do. That's the whole thing. One number, and she knows."

*Use when:* you want them to *want* the future. Duarte's "new bliss."

### 2. The single ask
One action, small enough to do this week, named precisely.

> "I'm asking for one thing: two engineers for six weeks. Not a budget line, not a
> committee. Two people, six weeks, and I'll come back with the answer."

*Use when:* the speech exists to get a decision. **Vagueness here voids the whole speech.**
"Support this initiative" is not an ask.

### 3. The callback
Return to your opening image, transformed by what came in between.

> "That night manager in Guangzhou still calls me sometimes. Last month he called at 2 a.m.
> to tell me the line had run for ninety days without stopping."

*Use when:* you opened with a scene. This is the most satisfying ending available in
English, and the reason is structural: the audience feels the shape close.

### 4. The statement of belief
Say what you believe, slowly, and stop.

> "I think this is the most important thing this team will do. I've thought so for two
> years. I still think so."

*Use when:* a rally, or any moment where conviction is the point. Delivery matters more
than words here — slow down, and do not add anything after it.

### 5. The reframe
Give them a new sentence to think with.

> "We've been treating this as a cost problem. It's a trust problem. Every number I showed
> you today is downstream of that."

*Use when:* your throughline is a reframe. It hands them the portable version of your idea.

### 6. The named person
End on the human being your argument is about.

> "Ashley is twenty-three. She's the reason I'm standing here."

*Use when:* you have an A-grade person in your material (Law 4). Ends on a face rather than
a concept, which is what memory actually stores.

## Never close with

- **A summary of what you just said.** The most common bad ending. Energy dies audibly, and
  you've spent your most valuable ten seconds on a table of contents.
- **"Any questions?"** as the actual last line. Deliver the close. Hold the silence. *Then*
  open the floor as a separate act.
- **A new argument.** If it mattered, it belonged earlier. If it didn't, it doesn't belong.
- **Trailing off.** "So… yeah. That's kind of it." Write the last sentence and memorize it.
- **Thanks as the whole ending.** "Thank you" is punctuation, not a close. It comes *after*
  the close, and it should be short.
- **An apology for time.** Nobody is grateful to be reminded you ran long.

---

## Mechanics that apply to both

**Write them first and last.** Draft the opening early — it forces you to know what your
talk is. Then rewrite it at the very end, once you know what the talk actually became. They
are almost never the same.

**Memorize verbatim.** Only these two stretches. The opening because nerves peak in the
first thirty seconds and autopilot must carry you through; the closing because it's the
line you'll be least able to improvise and most costly to fumble.

**Plant the callback deliberately.** Choose the phrase in the opening that you intend to
return to, and mark it in the script. Callbacks that happen by accident usually don't.

**Silence is part of both.** Before your first sentence: stand still, look at them, wait
two seconds. It reads as command of the room, and it settles your own breathing. After your
last sentence: stop. Don't fill it. The pause is what tells the audience to applaud, and
speakers who talk into it are the reason some rooms don't.

**Land the ending physically.** Stop moving. Feet still, hands down, eyes up. Audiences read
the body's punctuation before they process the sentence's.
