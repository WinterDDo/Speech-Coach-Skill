# Structural archetypes

Read this at Stage 3, every time. Different occasions need genuinely different skeletons.
Using a TED shape for a board update, or a pitch shape for a eulogy, is the most common
and most expensive structural mistake in this whole discipline.

## Choosing

| The audience's actual job in the room | Archetype | § |
|---|---|---|
| Decide whether to approve / fund / adopt something | **Persuasive Case** | 1 |
| Absorb a decision or status from someone accountable | **Executive Update** | 2 |
| Understand and be changed by an idea | **Idea Talk** | 3 |
| Learn what a new thing is and why they should care | **Launch** | 4 |
| Judge an investment opportunity | **Investor Pitch** | 5 |
| Mark an occasion, honor a person, share a moment | **Ceremonial** | 6 |
| Find out what went wrong and whether to keep trusting you | **Crisis / Apology** | 7 |
| Be moved to sustained effort | **Rally** | 8 |
| Acquire a skill or model they can use | **Teaching** | 9 |
| React well without preparation | **Impromptu** | 10 |

When two fit, pick by what the audience must *do next*, not by what the content is about.
A product launch to a board is an Executive Update wearing launch clothes.

## Reading the beat sheets

Percentages are of total time. **Stake** is 1–5: how much tension is live at that moment.
The stake column is the point — a healthy speech has a *shape*, and the most common
structural defect is a flat line at 2.

---

## 1. Persuasive Case
*Monroe's Motivated Sequence (Alan Monroe, Purdue, 1930s) — still the most reliable
persuasive skeleton ever built.*

**Use for:** proposals, recommendations, change initiatives, budget requests, advocacy.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | 10 | 3 | A scene, a number, or a question that makes them look up |
| **Need** | 25 | 5 | Make them *feel* the problem. Cost of the status quo, made concrete and personal to this room |
| **Satisfaction** | 30 | 3 | Your proposal, explained clearly enough to be evaluated |
| **Visualization** | 20 | 4 | Two futures: with, and without. Sensory, not abstract |
| **Action** | 15 | 4 | One specific ask, small enough to say yes to today |

**The trap.** Rushing Need to get to your solution. You are excited about the answer; they
have not yet agreed there's a question. Under-built Need is why good proposals die. If
you're cutting for time, cut Satisfaction, not Need.

**Note on Visualization.** Show the negative future *second* if you want caution, *first*
if you want hope. The one you end on is the one they carry.

---

## 2. Executive Update
*Minto's Pyramid Principle + SCQA. Answer first.*

**Use for:** board and exec updates, status reports, upward recommendations, steering
committees.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | 5 | 1 | The uncontested shared context. One or two sentences |
| **Complication** | 10 | 4 | What changed, what broke, what's now at risk |
| **Question** | 5 | 4 | The decision on the table, stated explicitly |
| **Answer** | 15 | 3 | Your recommendation, up front, in one sentence |
| **Support** | 45 | 2 | Exactly three arguments, each with evidence. MECE — no overlap, no gaps |
| **Ask + risks** | 20 | 3 | What you need, by when, and the two risks you're already watching |

**The trap.** Chronology. "First we did this, then we did that, and so we concluded…"
Executives will interrupt you at minute two and ask for the answer, and you'll deliver it
badly under pressure. Lead with it.

**Note.** This is the archetype where Law 2 (open a gap) bends. Senior audiences read
withheld conclusions as evasion. Keep the tension in the *Complication*, then resolve fast.
Also: assume you'll be interrupted. Build so the first ninety seconds stand alone.

---

## 3. Idea Talk
*Chris Anderson's TED framework, plus Duarte's oscillation.*

**Use for:** conference talks, TED-style, internal thought-leadership, guest lectures.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 8 | 4 | A concrete scene or a question with a wrong obvious answer |
| **Standing** | 7 | 2 | Why you. Woven into the story, not announced |
| **The gap** | 10 | 5 | What everyone believes, and why it's incomplete |
| **Build 1** | 20 | 3 | First step of the idea, with evidence and one instance |
| **Build 2** | 20 | 4 | Second step. Each build should produce a small "oh" |
| **Build 3** | 15 | 3 | Third step, the one that completes the picture |
| **Implication** | 12 | 4 | What follows if this is true — bigger than the topic |
| **Close** | 8 | 5 | The image. Callback to the hook |

**The trap.** Building a talk out of your *conclusions* instead of the *path you walked*.
Conclusions are a summary of thinking; the audience wants to think it with you. Give them
the wrong turns.

**Note.** Three builds is a ceiling, not a target. Two deep builds beat four shallow ones.

---

## 4. Launch
*The Jobs three-act structure.*

**Use for:** product launches, feature announcements, internal tool rollouts, demos.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The world today** | 10 | 3 | The problem everyone has stopped noticing because they're used to it |
| **The villain** | 10 | 5 | Name what's wrong. Status quo, an incumbent, a compromise everyone accepts |
| **The headline** | 5 | 4 | One sentence, tweet-length, that names the thing |
| **The reveal** | 10 | 5 | Show it. Silence. Let them look |
| **Demo as proof** | 30 | 3 | Do the thing live. One flow, done well, beats six features listed |
| **Three benefits** | 15 | 2 | Exactly three, in the customer's language, not the spec sheet's |
| **Availability** | 10 | 2 | Price, date, how to get it. Don't be coy |
| **One more thing** | 10 | 5 | Optional. Only if you actually have one |

**The trap.** Feature lists. Features are what it does; benefits are what changes for them.
Also: a demo that shows everything and lands nothing. Choose the single flow that makes the
villain visibly die.

---

## 5. Investor Pitch

**Use for:** fundraising, internal venture funding, partnership proposals.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | 12 | 4 | Whose pain, how expensive, how you know first-hand |
| **Why now** | 8 | 4 | What changed in the world that makes this possible today and not in 2019 |
| **Solution** | 15 | 3 | What you built, in one sentence plus one demonstration |
| **Traction** | 20 | 3 | Evidence it's working. Numbers with direction and time |
| **Market** | 10 | 2 | Bottom-up sizing. Top-down TAM slides invite disbelief |
| **Model** | 10 | 2 | How money is made and how unit economics move with scale |
| **Team** | 10 | 3 | Why this specific group wins this specific race |
| **Ask** | 15 | 4 | Amount, use of funds, what it buys in milestones |

**The trap.** Burying the ask, or leaving it vague. "We're raising a round" is not an ask.
Second trap: "Why now" omitted entirely — it's the question that separates a good idea
from a fundable one.

---

## 6. Ceremonial
*Aristotle's epideictic — the genre of praise, blame, and shared values.*

**Use for:** toasts, weddings, eulogies, award acceptance, retirements, anniversaries,
opening remarks.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim the relationship** | 10 | 2 | Who you are to this person or occasion. One line |
| **The scene** | 35 | 3 | *One* specific memory. Time, place, what was said. Small is better |
| **What it revealed** | 25 | 4 | What that moment shows about who they are |
| **The wider truth** | 15 | 3 | Why that quality matters to everyone in the room |
| **The wish** | 15 | 4 | Toast, blessing, or send-off. Direct address to them |

**The trap.** Adjective stacking. "Sarah is dedicated, kind, hardworking, and generous"
communicates almost nothing and is what everyone else will also say. **One true anecdote
outperforms ten adjectives**, every time, in every culture.

**Eulogy note.** Permission to include humor and imperfection — a person described only in
virtues stops being recognizable, and recognizability is the whole point. Grief is served
by accuracy. Also: write the last line first, and write it down. It is the line you will
be least able to improvise.

---

## 7. Crisis / Apology

**Use for:** incidents, outages, layoffs, recalls, public mistakes, missed commitments.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acknowledge** | 15 | 5 | State plainly what happened. No euphemism, no delay |
| **Take responsibility** | 15 | 5 | Active voice, first person. "We did this," not "this occurred" |
| **What we know / don't** | 20 | 4 | Facts confirmed, facts pending, and *when* you'll know more |
| **Impact on you** | 15 | 4 | Address the affected directly, in their terms |
| **Actions** | 25 | 3 | Specific, dated, assigned. Not "we'll do better" |
| **Commitment** | 10 | 3 | Next update, by when, on which channel |

**The trap.** The non-apology: "mistakes were made," "if anyone was offended," "we regret
that some customers experienced." Passive voice in an apology is heard as evasion, and it
is heard *immediately*.

**Sequencing note.** Do not explain before you acknowledge. Explanation that arrives first
is heard as excuse, and it poisons the acknowledgment that follows. Acknowledge, own,
*then* explain — if you explain at all.

## 8. Rally

**Use for:** all-hands, kickoffs, turnarounds, sales conferences, team moments before hard
stretches.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The hard truth** | 20 | 5 | Say the thing everyone knows and nobody says. This buys all your later credibility |
| **The cost of standing still** | 15 | 5 | What happens if nothing changes. Concrete |
| **Why us** | 20 | 3 | Evidence from this group's own history that they can do hard things |
| **The mission** | 20 | 4 | What we're going to do, in plain words |
| **Monday** | 15 | 3 | What each person does first. Specific enough to start |
| **Belief** | 10 | 5 | A statement of conviction, delivered slowly |

**The trap.** Starting with optimism. An audience that knows things are bad and hears
cheerfulness first concludes the speaker is either uninformed or lying, and stops
listening — permanently. **Name the hard truth first.** Churchill in 1940 opened by
describing a military catastrophe in unsparing detail; the defiance at the end works
*because* of the honesty at the start, not despite it.

---

## 9. Teaching

**Use for:** training, onboarding, workshops, technical explainers, lectures.

| Beat | % | Stake | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The failure case** | 10 | 4 | Show it going wrong first. Motivates the model |
| **The mental model** | 20 | 3 | The one idea, drawn or named. This is what they keep |
| **Worked example** | 25 | 2 | You do it, out loud, including the judgment calls |
| **Their turn** | 25 | 3 | They do it. Non-negotiable — without this, retention collapses |
| **Common mistakes** | 12 | 3 | The three errors you always see. Naming them prevents them |
| **One sentence** | 8 | 2 | The model, restated. The thing they write down |

**The trap.** Covering everything you know because you're the expert. Teaching is
subtraction. Also: explaining the model before showing why it's needed — the failure case
first is what makes the model feel like a relief instead of a chore.

---

## 10. Impromptu
*Carnegie's magic formula, and PREP.*

**Use for:** panels, Q&A, "would you say a few words," meeting interventions, hallway asks.

**PREP** — for answering a question:
> **Point.** Your answer in one sentence.
> **Reason.** Why.
> **Example.** One concrete instance.
> **Point.** Restate the answer.

**Carnegie's formula** — for a short persuasive remark, and it is very hard to beat:
> **Incident.** A specific thing that happened to you, told in detail.
> **Action.** What you want them to do.
> **Benefit.** What they get for doing it.

Note the proportions: the incident takes most of the time. Most people invert this and
spend ninety seconds on the ask and five on the story.

**Universal impromptu opener** when you need three seconds to think: repeat or reframe the
question out loud. It buys time, confirms you understood, and sounds deliberate.

## Compressing to the clock

The archetypes above assume ~10–20 minutes. Shorter formats aren't scaled-down versions —
beats *drop out*, they don't shrink proportionally.

| Time | What survives |
|---|---|
| **60 sec** | One beat. Point + one instance + ask. Nothing else fits |
| **3 min** | Three beats: gap, one build, close. Delete standing, delete implication |
| **5 min** | Four to five beats. One story, one argument, one ask |
| **10 min** | Full archetype, one build instead of three |
| **18–20 min** | Full archetype as written. The natural length for an Idea Talk |
| **45 min +** | Do not extend — *segment*. Three linked 12-minute movements with visible seams and a change of mode (demo, question, exercise) between them. Sustained one-directional attention decays badly past ~20 minutes |

**When cutting, cut whole beats.** A speech with all its beats at 60% length is worse than
one with 60% of its beats at full strength. The first sounds rushed everywhere; the second
sounds decisive.
