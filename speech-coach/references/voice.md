# Voice calibration

Read this at Stage 2, alongside material mining, and again before drafting.

A speech can satisfy every law in `principles.md` and still fail for one reason: it doesn't
sound like the person delivering it. This is the most common and most damaging failure of
machine-assisted speechwriting, and it is almost invisible on the page — it only appears
when a human stands up and says the words.

**Why it costs so much.** An audience detects register mismatch long before it can name it.
What they consciously register is not "these words aren't his" but *"I don't trust this
guy."* A speaker performing someone else's voice reads as insincere, and insincerity
discredits the argument. A plain, slightly awkward sentence the speaker actually owns
outperforms an elegant one they're visibly wearing.

## The specific trap in this skill

This skill studies King, Churchill, Lincoln, Jobs, and Obama. That creates a real pull
toward grandeur — anaphora, rising tricolons, moral cadence. **The teardowns are there for
structure, not for voice.** Almost nobody should sound like those five, and a mid-level
manager given convention-hall cadence in a Tuesday planning meeting is being set up to fail
in front of colleagues.

Default to the speaker's actual register. Reach for elevation only at the one or two
moments that have earned it.

## Building the voice profile

**Ask for a sample.** Any of these works, in order of usefulness:

1. A transcript or recording of them speaking — a previous talk, a podcast, a recorded
   meeting. Best source by far, because writing and speaking voices differ.
2. Something they wrote informally — a Slack message, an internal email, a note to their
   team. Second best.
3. Their answers in Stage 1 and 2. You already have these. **Mine them.** How someone
   answers "what went wrong that night" is a live sample of their narrative voice.

Never skip this because no sample was offered. Ask.

**Extract these ten dimensions:**

| Dimension | What to notice |
|---|---|
| **Sentence length** | Clipped and short, or long and rolling? |
| **Register** | Boardroom formal / professional plain / casual / blunt |
| **Pronoun** | "I" or "we"? Highly diagnostic, and people are consistent about it |
| **Humor** | None / dry / self-deprecating / warm / sharp |
| **Figurative language** | Do they reach for metaphor, or stay literal? |
| **Technical density** | Comfortable in jargon, or deliberately plain? |
| **Directness** | Do they state the ask, or circle it? |
| **Emotional range** | Understated, or expressive? |
| **Signature phrases** | Words they actually use. **Keep these** — they're free authenticity |
| **Profanity / slang** | What's in bounds for them, in this room |

## The lunch test

The single most useful technique in this document:

> **"Forget the speech for a second. Say it to me the way you'd say it to a colleague over
> lunch."**

Then write down what they say, close to verbatim. That is usually the sentence. People are
articulate, concrete, and rhythmically natural when they think they're talking rather than
composing — and they become stiff, abstract, and Latinate the moment they think they're
"writing a speech."

Most of the work of a speechwriter is catching people in the first mode and stopping them
from editing themselves into the second.

Use it whenever a passage has gone dead. Ask the question, listen, and replace the passage
with their answer.

## Rules for drafting in someone's voice

**Never write above their register.** If the speaker says "the numbers are bad," do not
hand them "we stand at an inflection point." The upgrade sounds better and performs worse.

**Stretch by one notch, not three.** A speech can be slightly more composed than the
speaker's everyday talk — that's what preparation buys. It should not debut a new
personality. If a line would make their team exchange glances, cut it.

**Preserve their tics.** If they say "look," or "the honest answer is," or "here's the
thing" — keep it. These are the fingerprints that make a script sound spoken. Sanding them
off is the most common way a draft becomes generically competent.

**Match, don't average.** Don't split the difference between their voice and a "good speech
voice." There is no such thing; there are only speakers who sound like themselves and
speakers who don't.

**Devices last.** Add rhetorical figures only where the argument peaks, and only if the
speaker can deliver them without embarrassment. Ask directly: *"Can you say this line
without wincing?"* A device the speaker doesn't believe in dies on stage.

## The read-back check

Before finishing any draft, read a paragraph back to the user and ask one question:

> **"Would you actually say this out loud?"**

Not "is this good?" — they'll be polite about that. "Would you say it?" gets an honest
answer, because they can picture themselves standing there.

Any "…sort of," "I guess," or hesitation is a no. Rewrite it with the lunch test. Do this
for the opening and the closing at minimum, since those are the parts they'll memorize
verbatim and the parts where borrowed voice is most exposed.

## When you're writing for someone you can't interview

Ghostwriting for an executive, a client, or a public figure with no access:

- Read or watch everything of theirs you can find. Three transcripts beats any amount of
  briefing from their staff.
- Note what they *never* do as carefully as what they do. Absences define a voice more
  sharply than habits.
- Write deliberately plainer than you think you should. A flat line in their register is
  recoverable; a soaring line in yours is not.
- Flag every place you guessed, and get those reviewed first: `[VOICE CHECK — is this how
  you'd put it?]`
- Build in a read-through with the actual speaker before rehearsal, and treat every line
  they change as data about the profile, not as a correction to argue with.
