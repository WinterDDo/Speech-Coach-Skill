# Five teardowns

Read this when the user needs to see how a master solved the problem they're facing, or
when an abstract law needs a worked example.

Full texts are not reproduced here — several are under copyright, and the structure is
what's instructive anyway. Short phrases are quoted for analysis. All five are easy to
find in full, and the user should read or watch the whole thing if one of them matches
their occasion.

Each teardown ends with **what not to copy**, which matters as much as the rest. These
were extraordinary occasions. Importing King's cadence into a quarterly review is not
homage; it's a category error, and rooms punish it hard.

---

## 1. Lincoln — Gettysburg Address (19 November 1863)

**Situation.** Dedication of a soldiers' cemetery four months after the battle. Edward
Everett, the celebrated orator of the day, spoke first for about two hours. Lincoln
followed with roughly 272 words and was done in about two minutes.

**Throughline.** *The dead have already consecrated this ground; the work left is ours.*

**Structure.** A clean three-part time frame:

| Beat | Move |
|---|---|
| **Past** | "Four score and seven years ago" — founds the nation on a proposition, not a territory |
| **Present** | "Now we are engaged in a great civil war" — the proposition is being tested |
| **Future** | "It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work" — the pivot from mourning to obligation |

**The moves.**

- **Tricolon of negation.** "We can not dedicate — we can not consecrate — we can not
  hallow." Three refusals, escalating, all monosyllabic Anglo-Saxon.
- **The famous tricolon** at the close: "of the people, by the people, for the people."
- **The dedication motif.** Some form of *dedicate / consecrate / devote* recurs
  throughout, binding a very short text into a single object.
- **Radical humility as strength.** "The world will little note, nor long remember what we
  say here" — spectacularly wrong, and the reason the line is remembered. Lowering the
  speech's own status raised it.
- **The inversion.** He arrives to dedicate the ground and argues the ground was already
  dedicated by the men who fought there. The audience's expected job is replaced with a
  harder one.

**What to steal.** The past/present/future frame is the most portable structure in this
document — it works for a company anniversary, a retirement, a turnaround, a founding
story. And the discipline: everything not load-bearing was removed.

**What not to copy.** The register. This diction in a modern room sounds like costume
drama. Steal the architecture, not the sentences.

## 2. Churchill — "We Shall Fight on the Beaches" (4 June 1940)

**Situation.** Delivered to the House of Commons days after the Dunkirk evacuation. The
British Army had escaped, having lost nearly all its equipment; invasion was plausible
within weeks. *(The widely-heard recording was made years later, in 1949 — the Commons
delivery was not broadcast.)*

**Throughline.** *This was a disaster, and we will not stop.*

**Structure.** The proportions are the lesson. The overwhelming majority of the speech is
sober military detail — losses, numbers, what went wrong, what was salvaged. The famous
passage is the last minute or so.

| Beat | Share | Move |
|---|---|---|
| The facts of the disaster | most of it | Unsparing. "Wars are not won by evacuations" |
| The sober assessment | | What can and cannot be expected next |
| The peroration | ~1 min | The anaphora, then the refusal to surrender |

**The moves.**

- **Honesty purchases the ending.** He refuses to let a rescue be mistaken for a victory.
  Because he told them the truth for twenty minutes, the defiance in the last minute is
  believed. Reverse the order and the whole thing is propaganda.
- **Accumulation** — one of the five elements he named in his own 1897 essay *The
  Scaffolding of Rhetoric*. "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing
  grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills."
  The repetition is the argument: it enacts refusal by refusing to stop.
- **Monosyllables at the peak.** *fight, beaches, fields, streets, hills.* Almost every
  word in the most famous passage is one syllable and Old English.
- **The de-escalation.** The passage ends on a longer, quieter sentence about the New World
  stepping forth to the rescue — the roar resolves rather than simply stopping.

**What to steal.** Name the hard truth first, in detail, before you ask anyone for
resolve. This is the single most useful thing in this document for anyone running a
turnaround, a layoff announcement, or a difficult all-hands. See `archetypes.md` §8.

**What not to copy.** The volume. This was an existential moment for a nation. A product
delay is not, and treating it that way destroys the speaker's judgment in the eyes of the
room.

## 3. King — "I Have a Dream" (28 August 1963)

**Situation.** The March on Washington, from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, to roughly
a quarter of a million people and a national television audience.

**Throughline.** *America made a promise it has not kept, and we are here to collect.*

**Structure.** Two halves, joined by a hinge that was not in the prepared text.

| Beat | Move |
|---|---|
| **The promissory note** | The founders signed a promise; Black Americans were handed "a bad check," returned marked "insufficient funds." A sustained financial metaphor |
| **The urgency** | "The fierce urgency of now" — against gradualism |
| **The discipline** | A warning against meeting physical force with physical force. This section is routinely forgotten and it is what makes the rest credible |
| **The dream** | Anaphora, eight-plus repetitions, escalating from national to intimately personal — his own children |
| **Let freedom ring** | Geographic accumulation across American mountains, borrowed from "My Country, 'Tis of Thee" |
| **Free at last** | Closes on a spiritual, in the voice of the tradition he's speaking from |

**The moves.**

- **One governing metaphor, fully sustained.** The check is not a simile in passing; it
  organizes the entire first half. Note what it does rhetorically: it reframes a demand for
  charity as a demand for *repayment*, which is a completely different moral claim.
- **The hinge.** The "dream" material was largely extemporaneous. By widely reported
  accounts, Mahalia Jackson called out "Tell them about the dream, Martin," and he left the
  text. He had used dream material in earlier speeches that year, so this was practiced
  improvisation, not invention — *he departed from a script into material he already owned.*
- **Escalation through anaphora.** Each "I have a dream" moves closer in — from the nation,
  to a state, to a hillside, to his own four children.
- **Borrowed authority by allusion.** Scripture, the Declaration, the Constitution, a
  patriotic hymn, a spiritual. He is never arguing alone; he speaks with the documents and
  songs the audience already reveres standing behind him.

**What to steal.** The governing metaphor, chosen for what it *reframes*. And the hinge
principle: know your material well enough that leaving the script means going somewhere
stronger, not somewhere unknown.

**What not to copy.** The cadence. It comes from a specific homiletic tradition, in a
speaker who had preached in it his entire life, at a moment of national reckoning. Imitated
outside all three, it reads as parody.

## 4. Jobs — Stanford Commencement (12 June 2005)

**Situation.** A commencement address, delivered about eighteen months after his first
cancer diagnosis.

**Throughline.** *You cannot connect the dots forward, so trust that following what you
love will make sense later.*

**Structure.** He states the architecture out loud in the first thirty seconds — three
stories, and he says so plainly. It's an unusually literal application of the "numbered
promise" (see `language.md`).

| Story | Content | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Connecting the dots** | Dropping out, sleeping on floors, sitting in on a calligraphy class | Trust that the dots connect later |
| **2. Love and loss** | Being fired from the company he founded, at thirty | Getting fired was the best thing that happened to him |
| **3. Death** | The cancer diagnosis and the day he expected to die | Death is the change agent; your time is limited |

**The moves.**

- **Announced structure.** Telling the audience the shape up front lowers their cognitive
  load and lets them relax into each story. Works because the stories themselves carry the
  tension.
- **Story as the entire argument.** No data, no framework, no slides. Three narratives,
  three sentences of lesson. The lessons alone would be greeting-card platitudes; the
  stories are what make them land — Law 4 at full strength.
- **Escalating stakes** — 1 to 5 across the three stories, ending on mortality. The order
  is not accidental.
- **Concreteness in the small details.** Returning Coke bottles for deposits, the free
  meals at the Hare Krishna temple. The specifics are what make it his life rather than an
  anecdote.
- **A borrowed close.** "Stay hungry. Stay foolish" — from the back cover of the final
  issue of the *Whole Earth Catalog*, credited on the spot. Four words, all monosyllables.

**What to steal.** Three stories, one lesson each, ordered by rising stakes. This is the
most copyable structure here for any personal or reflective talk — a retirement, a
farewell, a "what I've learned" talk.

**What not to copy.** The absence of an ask. Commencement addresses are epideictic; they
exist to mark an occasion. If your talk needs a decision, this structure will not get you
one.

## 5. Obama — Democratic National Convention Keynote (27 July 2004)

**Situation.** A state senator, largely unknown nationally, given a prime slot at his
party's convention.

**Throughline.** *My improbable story is only possible in a country that refuses to see
itself as divided.*

**Structure.** Standing first, argument second, and never separated.

| Beat | Move |
|---|---|
| **Standing** | His father herding goats in Kenya; his grandfather's service; his parents' "improbable love" and "abiding faith in the possibilities of this nation" |
| **The bridge** | "I stand here knowing that my story is part of the larger American story" — the personal is converted into the national |
| **The people** | Specific Americans, named, with specific problems |
| **The reframe** | The antithesis run: not a liberal America and a conservative America, not a Black America and a white America — the United States of America |
| **The close** | "The audacity of hope" — a phrase borrowed from a sermon, and later a book title |

**The moves.**

- **Ethos as narrative.** He never claims authority; he tells a story from which authority
  follows. Law 3, executed about as well as it gets.
- **Sustained antithesis** as the structural spine of the most famous passage. "Not X, but
  Y," repeated and escalated — the single best device for a reframe (`language.md`).
- **Audience as subject.** The speech is largely about *them*, populated by named ordinary
  people rather than by the speaker's positions.
- **A phrase built to be portable.** "The audacity of hope" is designed to be repeated by
  people who heard it once. Every speech should have one line engineered for that.

**What to steal.** Establish standing through a specific story rather than a credential
list, and do it before your first argument. And build one line short and strange enough to
be repeated.

**What not to copy.** The convention register — the cadence and the applause architecture
depend on a large partisan crowd trained to respond. In a boardroom it reads as
grandstanding.

## Reading across all five

| | Throughline in one line | Gap opened by | Standing from | Ends on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lincoln | Yes | An unfinished obligation | Office and shared grief | An image + a resolve |
| Churchill | Yes | An honestly stated disaster | Accountability for the war | A refusal |
| King | Yes | A promise unkept | A tradition he embodies | An image + a spiritual |
| Jobs | Yes | Three personal crises | Lived experience | A borrowed maxim |
| Obama | Yes | A country describing itself wrongly | A family story | A portable phrase |

**Every one of them:** one idea, a gap opened early, standing established from lived
experience, concrete over abstract, and an ending on an image or a demand — never a
summary.

**None of them:** opened with an agenda, listed credentials, used a slide, ended by
recapping, or ran long.
