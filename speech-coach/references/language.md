# Writing for the ear

Read this at Stage 4, and whenever editing at the line level.

Prose and speech are different crafts that happen to share an alphabet. A reader controls
the pace, can re-read a clause, and sees paragraph breaks. A listener has none of that.
They get one pass, in your order, at your speed. Every rule below follows from that single
asymmetry.

The fastest diagnostic in this entire skill: **read it aloud, standing up, at speaking
volume.** Every problem this document describes announces itself within ten seconds of
doing that. Nothing you can do silently on a screen substitutes for it.

---

## Sentence mechanics

**One idea per sentence.** If a sentence contains a "which" and an "although," it contains
two sentences that haven't been separated yet.

**Vary length on purpose.** Uniform sentence length — long or short — flattens into drone.
The pattern that works: build with two or three longer sentences, then land on a short one.

> "We spent eighteen months building a platform that could handle every case our largest
> customers could imagine, and we shipped it in March to genuine applause from the people
> who had asked for it. Then we watched the usage data for a quarter. Nobody used it."

The short sentence isn't decoration. It's where the meaning lands, and the long ones exist
to give it somewhere to fall from.

**Front-load the subject and verb.** English listeners hold an open clause at real
cognitive cost.

> Hard to hear: *"Because of the regulatory changes that took effect in January, which
> affected three of our five markets, our onboarding times increased."*
> Easy to hear: *"Our onboarding times went up. The cause was a regulatory change in
> January — it hit three of our five markets."*

**Twenty-five words is the practical ceiling** for a spoken sentence, and most should be
well under it. Not because long sentences are wrong, but because they must be *deliberate*
— a long sentence should be a choice you can defend, and it needs a breath after it.

**Active voice, named actors.** "The decision was made" hides who decided, and audiences
notice the hiding even when they can't name what they noticed. In an apology it's fatal
(see `archetypes.md` §7).

## Diction

Prefer the short, concrete, Anglo-Saxon word. This is centuries-old advice in English
oratory and it holds because those words are shorter, older, and learned earlier — they
land without a decoding step.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| implement | do, build, start |
| terminate | end, stop, kill |
| facilitate | help, run |
| leverage | use |
| optimize | improve, speed up |
| commence | begin, start |
| endeavor | try |
| procure | buy, get |
| approximately | about |
| in order to | to |
| at this point in time | now |
| subsequent to | after |
| a significant proportion of | most, half, two-thirds |

Churchill's most quoted line offers "blood, toil, tears and sweat" — four monosyllables,
all Old English. The Latinate version ("hemorrhage, exertion, lachrymation and
perspiration") is the same content and no speech at all.

**Kill jargon by translation, not deletion.** Don't remove a technical term the audience
needs. Say it, then immediately say it again in plain words: *"We had a cache invalidation
problem. The system was confidently serving answers that had stopped being true."*

## The device toolkit

These are not ornaments. They're load-bearing structure that the ear can detect (see
`principles.md` Law 5). Use them at the moments that matter — a device on a trivial
sentence spends credibility for nothing.

**Tricolon (rule of three).** Three items, ideally ascending in weight.
> "of the people, by the people, for the people"
*Use at:* the thesis, the summary of your argument, the close. **Three is the smallest
number that makes a pattern.** Two is a comparison; four is a list.

**Anaphora.** Repeat the opening words across successive clauses.
> "We shall fight on the beaches. We shall fight on the landing grounds. We shall fight in
> the fields…"
*Use at:* your emotional peak, and once at most. Three to five repetitions. Beyond that it
becomes a chant, which is a different and riskier instrument.

**Epistrophe.** Repeat the *ending* words instead. Rarer, and lands harder in a small room.
> "…this project is late. The reason we lost the account is that this project is late."

**Antithesis.** Set two things against each other. Meaning arrives faster through contrast
than definition.
> "Not because it is easy, but because it is hard."
> "We don't have a technology problem. We have a decision problem."
*Use at:* the moment you reframe the issue. This is the single best device for a pivot.

**Chiasmus.** Antithesis with the terms crossed. Memorable, and easy to overdo — one per
speech, maximum.
> "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country."

**Asyndeton.** Drop the conjunctions for speed and finality.
> "We tested it, we shipped it, we were wrong."

**Polysyndeton.** Add conjunctions for accumulation and weight.
> "And the servers went down, and the phones started ringing, and the status page was
> wrong, and nobody could reach the on-call engineer."

**Anadiplosis.** End a clause with the word that starts the next. Builds a chain.
> "It cost us a quarter. The quarter cost us the roadmap. The roadmap cost us the year."

**The governing metaphor.** One extended metaphor, sustained across the whole speech,
outperforms six clever unrelated ones. King's "promissory note" — America wrote a check
marked insufficient funds — runs as an organizing image, not a passing simile.
*Rule:* pick one, commit, return to it at the close. Mixed metaphors are a sign the
speaker doesn't have one.

**The callback.** Plant a phrase in the opening. Return to it in the last thirty seconds,
changed by everything in between. This is the highest return-on-effort device in the
toolkit — it produces the audible feeling that a speech has *closed*. If you do one
sophisticated thing in a speech, do this.

**Concession.** "You're right that this will cost us two months. Here's why I'm asking for
them anyway." Conceding the real objection is what makes the rest of your argument sound
like reasoning rather than advocacy.

## Numbers out loud

Numbers are hostile to the ear. Handle them deliberately.

- **Never more than three numbers in a row.** After the third, the audience stops
  processing and starts waiting.
- **Round aggressively.** "Forty-two thousand, one hundred and sixteen" is noise. "Just
  over forty thousand" is information. Precision is for the appendix.
- **Scale by comparison, not by magnitude.** "1.2 million cubic meters" means nothing;
  "five hundred Olympic pools" means something. Compare to a thing in the room if you can.
- **One number per beat, maximum, if it matters.** The number you want remembered should
  be alone.
- **Say the direction, not just the value.** "Churn is 8%" is a fact. "Churn doubled in
  two quarters, to 8%" is an argument.

## Signposting

The listener can't see your structure, so you have to say it.

- **Numbered promises.** "There are three things I want you to take away." Then *actually
  say "the second thing"* when you get there. Promising three and delivering an unmarked
  blur is worse than not promising.
- **Turn signals.** "That's the problem. Here's what we're going to do about it." Short,
  explicit, at every major seam.
- **The occasional forward reference.** "I'll come back to that number in a minute" — then
  do. This creates a small open loop, which is Law 2 at the paragraph level.
- **Don't signpost trivia.** "Next, I'd like to briefly mention…" is throat-clearing. Only
  mark the seams that matter.

## The ban list

These phrases are dead on arrival. Cut on sight.

**Openings:** "I'm excited to be here." / "Before I begin…" / "Can everyone hear me?" /
"Without further ado." / "In today's fast-paced world." / "Webster's dictionary defines…"

**Middles:** "As you can see on this slide." / "I know I'm running short on time, so…" /
"To be honest with you" (implying the rest wasn't). / "Basically." / "At the end of the
day." / "Let me unpack that." / "It goes without saying" (then don't say it). /
"I won't bore you with the details" (then either bore them or omit them silently).

**Endings:** "So, yeah, that's it." / "I think I'm out of time." / "Any questions?" as the
actual final line — deliver your close, hold the beat, *then* open the floor.

**Anywhere:** apologizing for your own talk, your slides, your voice, or your nerves. The
audience hadn't noticed, and now they're watching for it.

## Writing to be interpreted

If the talk goes through an interpreter, most decisions above change. Find out *which mode*
before drafting a word — the two are not variations of each other.

**Consecutive** (you speak, then the interpreter speaks): **halve the content.** This is
arithmetic, not style. Eight minutes of stage time is four minutes of your words. Speakers
who ignore this get cut off mid-argument, every time.

**Simultaneous** (interpreter in a booth, real time): the clock holds, but the interpreter
runs a few seconds behind you and is translating your *previous* sentence while you speak
the next. Everything that depends on precise timing lands late or not at all.

**Rules for both:**

- **Complete, short units.** Interpreters work in chunks. A sentence that suspends its verb
  or its point to the very end forces them to either wait or guess — and between languages
  with different word order, guessing is where errors come from. Front-load subject and verb
  even more aggressively than usual.
- **No idioms, no puns, no wordplay, no sports metaphors.** "Hit it out of the park,"
  "moving the goalposts," "the elephant in the room" — these either die or get rendered
  literally, which is worse. Say the plain thing.
- **Send the script or outline to the interpreter in advance.** The single highest-leverage
  thing you can do, and it costs one email. Include a written list of every **number, name,
  and technical term** — misheard numbers are the most common interpretation error by a wide
  margin.
- **Assume verbal humor dies.** Situational humor — something absurd that actually happened
  — often survives, because the funny part is the event, not the phrasing.
- **Pause at chunk boundaries, not mid-clause.** Your pause map is doing double duty now:
  it's also the interpreter's working rhythm.
- **Never speed up to make time.** You can accelerate; the interpreter cannot. Cut a beat
  instead — which is why pre-marked `[CUT IF SHORT]` matters more here than anywhere.
- **Simple devices survive; ornate ones don't.** Anaphora and the rule of three usually
  carry across. Chiasmus, alliteration, and anything that depends on the sound of a
  particular English word will not. Put your effort into structure and concreteness, which
  translate perfectly.
- **Look at the audience, not the interpreter.** The interpreter is a channel, not your
  listener.

## Read-aloud checklist

Standing, at volume, with a timer.

1. Did you stumble anywhere? → That sentence is broken. Rewrite, don't re-attempt.
2. Did you run out of breath? → Too long. Split it.
3. Did you get bored saying it? → They'll be more bored hearing it. Cut the beat.
4. Could you say any sentence from memory after reading it once? → If not, it's too
   complex to be *heard* once either.
5. Where did your energy drop? → That's the beat that doesn't belong to you. Either find
   real material for it or delete it.
6. What's the actual runtime versus the limit? → Must land under 85%.

Run `scripts/speech_check.py` for the mechanical measures. Do this list for everything
the script can't see, which is most of what matters.
